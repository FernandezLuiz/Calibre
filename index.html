01 / The Basis

Theory + Practice

Message design exists between theory and practice. Theory provides concepts and principles, while practice tests how those principles function in real communication situations.

Neither can operate effectively in isolation. Design becomes meaningful when theoretical knowledge is translated into decisions that help receivers find, perceive, understand and use information.

Theory ↔ Practice
02 / The Process

Communication is a process.

A message begins with a sender and an intention. That intention is transformed into a representation, communicated through a medium and interpreted by a receiver.

Feedback makes communication cyclical rather than purely linear. The receiver's response can influence the sender and future versions of the message.

Sender → Intention → Representation → Receiver

03 / Design Disciplines

Three selected disciplines

01

Graphic Design

Graphic design organises visual elements such as typography, images, symbols, colour and composition into a coherent visual form.

02

Information Design

Information design focuses on how content is selected, structured and presented so that information can be accessed and understood efficiently.

03

Instruction Design

Instruction design considers how information can support learning, action and the development of knowledge or skills.

Message Design also includes Mass Design and Persuasion Design.
16 PRINCIPLES / 4 GROUPS
16

Pettersson organises sixteen Message Design principles into four groups: functional, administrative, aesthetic and cognitive.

The principles are supported by practical guidelines that translate theoretical concepts into design decisions.

150 PRACTICAL GUIDELINES

Functional

01—06
01

Defining the Problem

Before designing the message, the sender, intended receiver, purpose and requirements need to be analysed. The designer must also determine which medium is most suitable for the message.

02

Providing Structure

Information should be organised so that relationships between elements become clear. A coherent structure helps the receiver navigate the message.

03

Providing Clarity

Content should stand out clearly from its background. Text, images, symbols and colour should be easy to distinguish and as clear and unambiguous as possible.

04

Providing Simplicity

Content and presentation should be adapted to the intended receiver. Simplicity in text, images and graphic form makes information easier to perceive, process and remember.

05

Providing Emphasis

Clear contrast and specific visual elements can direct attention towards important information. Typography and layout help establish hierarchy.

06

Providing Unity

Consistent typography, layout, terminology and highlighting create an overall sense of unity. Inconsistency can make a message more difficult to interpret.

Administrative

07—10
07

Information Access

Information should be easy to locate and access when the receiver needs it. Both the information set and the relevant content within it should support efficient retrieval.

08

Information Costs

The designer must consider the costs of designing and producing an information set. Production, distribution and storage should also be planned early in the process.

09

Information Ethics

Information designers must respect copyright, laws and regulations related to the design, production, distribution, storage and use of information.

10

Securing Quality

Information should be correct, credible and relevant to the intended audience. High-quality material should have a clear structure and remain legible, readable and understandable.

Aesthetic

11—12
11

Harmony

Design elements should fit together and form harmonious relationships. Typography, layout and other visual decisions should function as one coherent whole.

12

Aesthetic Proportion

Proportion can have a mathematical basis, while aesthetic judgement remains subjective. Designers should consider the preferences and expectations of the intended receiver.

Cognitive

13—16
13

Facilitating Attention

The designer must first attract the receiver's attention and then help maintain it. Relevant information should stand out without unnecessary visual competition.

14

Facilitating Perception

Text, images, colour, symbols and layout should work together as a meaningful whole. Information that attracts attention must also be easy to perceive and interpret.

15

Facilitating Mental Processing

Information should be simple, clear and unambiguous for the intended audience. The designer must consider the receiver's knowledge when deciding how much complexity to present.

16

Facilitating Memory

Simplicity supports memory as well as perception and processing. Clear and meaningful information is easier to understand, retain and recall.

CASE STUDIES / APPLICATION

Theory becomes practice.

CASE STUDY 01 / WAYFINDING

Eliminate visual noise.

Wayfinding is a practical test of message design in complex physical spaces such as airports and transit hubs. Under time pressure, users need directional information that is immediately visible, understandable and easy to follow.

Clarity, simplicity and emphasis reduce unnecessary visual competition. Important directional information should stand out through hierarchy and contrast.

CASE STUDY 02 / DATA VISUALIZATION

Make relationships visible.

Data visualisation transforms quantitative information into a form that can be compared, interpreted and understood. When too many visual elements compete for attention, the underlying data becomes harder to read.

Structure, clarity, simplicity and emphasis help establish hierarchy. Redundant decoration can be reduced while contrast is reserved for the most relevant information.

CASE STUDY 03 / EDITORIAL ARCHITECTURE

Editorial rhythm.

Editorial design is not only about individual pages but also about the relationship between pages and spreads. Changes in scale, density, typography and white space create rhythm throughout a publication.

Dense sections can slow the reading experience, while large statements and generous white space create moments of emphasis and transition. A consistent grid connects these variations.
